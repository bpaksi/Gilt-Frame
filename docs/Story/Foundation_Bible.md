# The Gilt Frame — Foundation Bible

**The rebuilt canon. This is the single source of truth for the story.**

Where this document and any older lore disagree, this wins. It supersedes the vague, mystical version of the Order (the "Sight as magic power" material) and the forked chapter maps. It grounds the whole fiction in real, checkable history, which is what a Dan Brown story runs on. The private emotional layer (Story 2, the love letter, the fragments) lives in the Story Spine and is referenced here but not repeated.

A note on structure: everything below is written to be true twice. Story 1 is what Christine experiences and believes. Story 2 is what is actually true, and it stays hidden until the end. Both readings live in the same words.

---

## 1. The founding anchor: the White City

The Order claims to be ancient, and it never proves it. That vagueness is deliberate and stays. But the story's entire trail of evidence begins at one real, concrete place, and this is the anchor everything hangs from:

**The World's Columbian Exposition, Chicago, 1893. The White City.**

The real facts, all verifiable, all usable:

- A temporary city of gleaming white palaces was built on the lakefront for a single summer. The buildings were not marble. They were staff, a plaster and fiber skin over wood and steel, painted white and lit with the new electric light. It was breathtaking, and it was built to be taken down. Most of it burned in 1894.
- **Bertha Palmer** was President of the Board of Lady Managers. She used that position to commission and champion women artists.
- She commissioned **Mary Cassatt** to paint a mural for the Woman's Building. It was called **Modern Woman**, roughly 58 feet wide, and its central panel showed young women plucking the fruits of knowledge and passing them to the next generation.
- **The mural vanished after the fair and has never been found.** No record of its destruction survives. This is a genuine, unsolved historical mystery.

This is the perfect foundation for this particular game, because the White City *is* the theme, already made real: the most beautiful thing anyone had ever built, made knowing it would not last, and gone within a year. The Order did not have to invent an emblem for impermanence. History handed it one.

**The canon:** the Order did not begin at the White City, but it *crystallized* there. The fair was the Order's great act, a whole city raised as a lesson in seeing beauty before it goes. The lost mural is the Order's holy relic, the one fragment it has been trying to recover ever since. The search for what the mural encoded is the spine of the game.

## 2. What the Order is

**Story 1 (what Christine believes):** an old fellowship of artists, patrons, and makers who hide truths inside art and architecture, preserve a way of seeing they call the Sight, and recruit the rare person who already sees that way. Its members have been "taken" across the centuries by an enemy that wants the Sight extinguished. The conspiracy is real. The danger is real.

**Story 2 (the truth, GM only, never surfaced):** there is no Order and no enemy organization. The artists really lived and really died. What took them was mortality, and what the story is really about is the fear that keeps most people from living the way those artists lived. The full architecture of this, and the ending where Christine recognizes it without being told, lives in the **Story Spine** document. Do not surface any of it in player-facing copy.

---

## 3. What the Order protects (sharpened)

The old canon called it "the Sight" and left it a mystical, undefined power. For a Dan Brown story that is too soft, because the riddles need something concrete to point at. So the definition is sharpened without losing the mystery:

**The Sight is not a power. It is a practice: the ability to see what is there and will not stay.**

It is attention, not magic. It is what Monet was doing when he painted the same haystack thirty times, recording how one fleeting thing changes with the light. It is checkable in the real world, which is exactly why it can anchor puzzles: the Order can direct Christine's gaze to a real detail on a real canvas, and either she sees it or she does not.

What the Order "protects," then, is not a treasure. It is the practice itself, carried forward through the works and places where it was encoded. The lost mural is the greatest of these, because its very subject was the passing of knowledge to the next generation. The Order protects the chain, and Christine is being drawn into it as its next link.

## 4. The pluggable doctrine (the engine, as canon)

This is the mechanic that makes the game adaptable, and it is written into the fiction so it is a story rule, not just a production trick.

The Order moves through **real people** and **real places**. Each chapter, Christine identifies a real historical figure through their work at a real location, and a real fact about that person becomes the key that points to the next person and place.

Two kinds of people:

- **Members** knowingly encoded the practice into their work (the canon names Cassatt and Monet among these).
- **Carriers** saw and recorded without understanding what they had found. The Order watched them and made sure the work survived.

The connective thread between a person and a place can be one of two things, and this is the doctrine that unlocks everything:

- **A true link.** Palmer really did shape the Art Institute's collection. Cassatt's works really are gathered at certain museums. History records the connection, and the Order simply claims it always knew.
- **An Order attribution.** A real person paired with a real place they were never actually associated with. History records no link. The Order claims a hidden one. *"The Order's records do not match the world's. Where the world remembers nothing, the Order remembers a reason."*

This is what makes the fact-and-fiction blur a feature instead of a liability, and it is the freedom that lets the game follow your real travel. When a trip takes Christine somewhere Palmer or Cassatt never set foot, the Order supplies the missing connection, and Christine can never fully verify or disprove it, which is precisely what sustains the mystery.

**The one hard authoring rule that keeps it credible:** every claim is anchored to at least one real, checkable fact, and only the *connection or the meaning* is invented. Never invent a "fact" a curious player could disprove by reading the placard on the wall. The real fact is the seduction. The fictional link is the mystery. Break that rule and the whole spell collapses, because the moment she catches the Order in a checkable lie, she stops believing all of it.

## 6. The Sparrow

Christine's name within the Order. It is kept, because it works, and because its real-world hook is genuine: the man who built the manor, the cereal magnate who fed the world, also famously fed the sparrows. "The man who fed the world also fed the sparrows. Consider that, young bird."

What the sparrow means in the Order's mouth: small, alert, common, overlooked by those who do not know what to look for, and yet it survives where larger creatures cannot. She was not given the name. She revealed it.

The name ascends as she proves worthy, through a ladder of birds, never announced, simply used. The full progression and its timing live in the Story Spine. The rule here: the Order never says "you have been promoted." It just begins addressing her differently, and trusts her to notice.

## 7. The Marker

The Order's sigil and its calling card. It appears on every message to the Sparrow. It is also the entire theme compressed into one symbol, which is why the visual design will be built around it (see the Tone north star).

A rectangular **gilt frame**. Inside it, an **hourglass**, drawn as two flowing curves that cross at the center. A **single grain above**, the fleeting present, one moment of light about to fall. **Three grains below**, gathered and still, the eternal past, the settled dead.

Its meaning, plainly: the light will not last. When the world looks at a framed painting, it sees art. When the Order looks, it sees a door, and a reminder that the threshold between seeing and passing by is always right there, and always closing.

## 8. The unnamed enemy

**Story 1:** the Order is losing. Its gifted are "taken." The pattern is deliberate and it escalates across the game: Kellogg blinded, Cassatt blinded, Monet's sight failing, Palmer taken in 1918. Blinding is the enemy's preferred method because it is quiet. The enemy is never named, because naming a thing gives it purchase. It could be anywhere. It could be inevitable.

**Story 2 (GM only):** the enemy is mortality, and the fear of it. The blindings and deaths are real biography, mourned rather than avenged. The escalation curve and the reframe belong to the Story Spine.

The one craft note that matters for the whole game: keep the danger *implied* and let each repetition of the blindness pattern add a new dimension, not just another name. The dread has to build toward the ending, where it is reframed, so it must never detonate into anything literal along the way.

---

## 9. Canon guardrails

The rules that keep the fiction airtight, and keep the ending ("she knows without being told") possible:

- Never confirm who is behind the game. Never name the enemy. Deflect every meta-question in the Order's voice, never defensively.
- Never invent a disprovable fact. Anchor every claim to something real; invent only the connection and the meaning. (Section 4.)
- The Oracle draws only from this canon and what Christine has unlocked. It cannot reference what she has not yet earned.
- The Order never breaks character, all the way to the end. The recognition at the finale happens inside Christine, not because the Order confesses. (See the Spine.)
- No em dashes in player-facing copy. Commas instead.
